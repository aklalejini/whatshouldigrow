A chewed leaf is not a diagnosis. Before treating a garden pest problem, identify what is doing the damage, decide whether the damage is serious, and choose the least disruptive control that will actually work.

That is the home-garden version of integrated pest management, or IPM. It is not anti-spray and it is not spray-first. It is a sequence: observe, identify, set a threshold, prevent what you can, then treat only when needed.

Aphids, cabbage worms, hornworms, squash bugs, cucumber beetles, flea beetles, slugs, and spider mites all call for different responses. So do deer, rabbits, voles, and groundhogs. Treating all damage as "bugs" wastes time and often kills beneficial insects that were helping.

## Decide whether action is needed

Some damage is cosmetic. A few holes in mature kale leaves are not the same as cut seedlings, collapsing squash vines, or fruit with larvae inside. The goal is not a garden with no insects. The goal is keeping damage below the point where the plant, crop, or harvest is meaningfully harmed.

Useful questions:

- Is the plant still growing strongly?
- Is the pest population increasing or already fading?
- Is the edible part being damaged?
- Are beneficial insects present?
- Is the crop young enough that small damage could set it back?

For uncertain cases, take photos, check leaf undersides, visit the garden after dark, and ask a county extension office or Master Gardener program for help with identification.

## Prevent the predictable problems

Physical exclusion is often the cleanest pest control in a vegetable garden. Row cover over brassicas prevents cabbage white butterflies from laying eggs. Insect netting over young cucurbits can reduce cucumber beetle and squash bug pressure before flowering. Fencing is the real answer for deer and rabbits where pressure is high.

Timing also helps. In some regions, spring brassicas are easier than fall brassicas. Butternut and tromboncino squash usually tolerate squash vine borer pressure better than zucchini. Crop rotation, cleanup of diseased debris, and wider spacing reduce repeat problems.

## Use targeted controls when prevention is not enough

Start with low-disruption options:

- Hand-pick hornworms, squash bugs, Japanese beetles, and slugs when numbers are manageable.
- Use a strong water spray for aphids on sturdy plants.
- Use insecticidal soap for soft-bodied pests when direct contact is possible.
- Use Bt kurstaki for caterpillars on brassicas and similar crops.
- Use iron phosphate baits for slugs where hand-picking is not keeping up.

Read labels, avoid spraying open flowers, and apply in the evening when a product has pollinator risk while wet. More pesticide is not better IPM; correct pesticide, correct pest, correct timing is the point.

## Build a garden that helps you

Many pest outbreaks settle faster when the garden has predators and parasitoids already present. Let some herbs flower. Plant small-flowered companions such as dill, cilantro, alyssum, yarrow, and native asters. Keep soil covered. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides unless there is a clear, serious reason.

A balanced garden will still have pests. It should also have lady beetles, lacewings, hoverflies, parasitic wasps, ground beetles, spiders, birds, and other helpers. Give them a reason to stay.
