Most plant failures start before planting day. The tag says the plant is hardy. The nursery pot looks healthy. The garden bed has an empty spot. What gets missed is whether that spot actually matches the plant.

"Right plant, right place" is a simple rule, but it works because it is specific. Match the plant to the site's light, soil, water, climate, and mature size. The closer the match, the less correction you have to do later.

## Read the site before choosing the plant

Watch the planting area at several times of day. Morning sun, afternoon sun, reflected heat, and high shade all behave differently. A plant that handles full sun in a cool climate may still struggle against a west-facing wall in a hot one.

Use plain categories, then add context:

- Full sun: six or more hours of direct sun.
- Part sun: roughly four to six hours, often with better growth when some direct sun is present.
- Part shade: similar hours, but protected from harsh afternoon exposure.
- Shade: less than four hours of direct sun or mostly filtered light.

Take a few photos at 9 a.m., noon, and 4 p.m. They are more useful than memory when you are standing at a nursery bench later.

## Check soil and drainage

Soil texture affects water, roots, and nutrients. Clay holds water and can drain slowly. Sand drains quickly and may dry out. Loam sits between them. Organic matter helps most soils, but it does not turn a wet clay pocket into a Mediterranean herb bed overnight.

For drainage, dig a hole about a foot deep, fill it with water, let it drain, then fill it again and time the second drain. Standing water many hours later means wet-site plants or drainage work, not lavender and rosemary.

Use a soil test for pH and nutrients before adding amendments. Blueberries, azaleas, and rhododendrons need acidic soil. Many vegetables prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil. Guessing at pH is a common way to waste a season.

## Use USDA zone as the floor, not the whole answer

USDA hardiness zones describe average annual extreme minimum temperature. They matter for winter survival, especially for trees, shrubs, vines, and perennials.

They do not tell you everything a plant needs. Summer heat, humidity, wind, freeze-thaw cycles, rainfall pattern, soil drainage, and local frost pockets can matter just as much. For long-lived plants, check regional extension notes and observe what is thriving within a few miles of you.

## Believe the mature size

Most shrubs and trees are sold young. That does not make them small plants. A shrub listed at six feet wide needs real room. A tree planted under a power line or two feet from a foundation becomes a pruning problem forever.

Before buying, ask:

- How wide will this plant get without hard pruning?
- Will it block windows, paths, driveways, or sight lines?
- Will roots, thorns, fruit drop, or litter create a maintenance issue?
- Is there enough air movement around the mature plant?

Plants usually look better when they are allowed to grow into their natural form.

## Match the plant to the job

A front foundation bed needs structure in winter, not just flowers in May. A patio bed may need summer fragrance, shade tolerance, or plants that do not drop fruit onto seating. A driveway strip needs plants that can handle heat, salt, and reflected light. A bed under mature trees needs dry-shade plants that can compete with roots.

Write the job down before shopping. "Evergreen screening in part sun, clay soil, low water after establishment" produces a better plant list than "something pretty."

## Choose natives with local range in mind

Native plants can support local insects and birds, but "native" is still regional. A plant native to the eastern United States may not be native to your county or ecoregion. Straight species, selections, and cultivars may also differ in wildlife value.

Use state extension resources, native plant societies, and invasive species lists. Avoid plants known to escape gardens in your region, even if they are easy to buy.

## A nursery checklist

Before buying, answer these:

1. How many hours of direct sun does the planting spot get?
2. What is the soil texture and drainage?
3. What is the soil pH if the plant is pH-sensitive?
4. Is the plant hardy in your zone and realistic for your summer climate?
5. What are the mature height and width?
6. Does the plant match the job of the space?
7. Is it invasive or discouraged in your state?

If you cannot answer those yet, take a photo of the tag and check at home. That pause saves money.
